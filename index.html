Best NGO · 2027 Finalist

Breadline Africa is replacing
unsafe school pit toilets

About this finalist

CategoryBest NGO
RepresentativeJohan Nel
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

A toilet is part of the infrastructure a child relies on to get through a school day safely. For learners using a deteriorating pit latrine, even a routine visit can carry serious risk. Breadline Africa’s Safe Sanitation Programme replaces unsafe school toilets with facilities designed to protect learners’ safety, dignity and access to education.

Founded in 1993, Breadline Africa works to ensure that children have access to the basic infrastructure needed to learn, develop and reach their full potential. Operating across all nine provinces, the organisation partners with school governing bodies, provincial education departments and local contractors to deliver essential facilities to low-quintile schools and early childhood development (ECD) centres. Its projects include classrooms, libraries, kitchens and sanitation facilities, addressing some of the fundamental inequalities that affect children's access to quality education.

Its current national priority is the Safe Sanitation Programme, launched in 2023 following the tragic death of a child who fell into a school pit latrine. The incident highlighted a devastating reality: thousands of South African schools continue to rely on unsafe pit toilets that are full, deteriorating or at risk of collapse. Beyond the immediate danger to children's lives, inadequate sanitation affects their health, dignity and ability to participate fully in education. Girls may miss school during menstruation, while learners with disabilities can face additional barriers to accessing facilities that are not designed to accommodate their needs.

Through the Safe Sanitation Programme, Breadline Africa is working to replace dangerous pit toilets with safe, hygienic and accessible sanitation facilities. The initiative recognises that sanitation is not simply an infrastructure issue, but a fundamental educational right. By providing appropriate facilities and implementing rigorous monitoring processes, the organisation aims to create school environments where children can learn without compromising their safety or dignity.

Leading this work is Johan Nel, whose commitment to educational infrastructure stems from witnessing first-hand the conditions in which many South African children are expected to learn. For Nel, the importance of the organisation's work lies in understanding that the physical environment in which a child receives an education can profoundly influence their future. Having visited schools where learners must effectively choose between their safety and their education, he believes that providing essential infrastructure is one of the most meaningful and enduring contributions an organisation can make.

Under his leadership, Breadline Africa has experienced significant growth. In 2014, the organisation had an annual income of approximately R20 million and had completed around 250 projects. Today, its annual income exceeds R100 million, with more than 2,500 completed projects benefiting 358,408 children and community members. This growth has enabled the organisation to expand its reach and respond to increasingly urgent infrastructure needs.

The Safe Sanitation Programme has already delivered encouraging results, with 1,045 safe toilets installed, reaching more than 18,777 learners and staff. Another defining moment in the organisation's history came during the COVID-19 pandemic, when school closures left many vulnerable families without access to essential support. Breadline Africa adapted its operations to provide emergency food relief, demonstrating its ability to respond to changing community needs while remaining committed to its wider mission.

For Nel, one of the most significant aspects of Breadline Africa's work is its lasting impact. Unlike many interventions that require ongoing delivery, infrastructure can continue serving communities for decades. Every classroom, library and safe toilet represents an investment in future generations, including children the organisation may never meet.

Replacing a toilet block also changes how a school can serve its learners. Facilities need to accommodate children who require accessible toilets and learners who need privacy during menstruation. Monitoring is part of the programme because the value of a new installation depends on how it serves the school over time. For Breadline Africa, safe sanitation belongs alongside classrooms and early learning spaces in any investment in education.

Best NGO · 2027 Finalist

How The Health Foundation
connects funding to public services

About this finalist

CategoryBest NGO
RepresentativeHarry Grainger
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

An organ donation campaign and a project using routine health data to improve tuberculosis and HIV services call for different kinds of expertise. Both need organisations that can manage funds, coordinate partners and keep projects accountable. The Health Foundation of South Africa (THF) fills that role, linking government priorities, donor resources and the teams doing the work.

Established in 2011 as an independent non-profit company and public benefit organisation, THF recognises that meaningful, lasting change requires more than financial investment. It requires effective partnerships, responsible resource management and strong institutions capable of translating funding into measurable outcomes. The Foundation occupies an important position between funding, strategy and implementation, helping organisations and public-sector partners deliver programmes effectively and sustainably.

Through resource mobilisation, financial oversight, sound governance, strategic guidance and stakeholder engagement, THF creates an enabling environment in which health and social development initiatives can succeed. Its work extends beyond providing funding, focusing instead on strengthening the systems, relationships and accountability structures that allow programmes to deliver lasting benefits.

Leading this work is Chief Executive Officer Harry Grainger, whose passion for the non-profit sector is rooted in a belief that health and wellness are fundamental to human potential. With many years of experience in the sector, Grainger is driven by the opportunity to bring people, resources and partnerships together to improve lives and strengthen communities. Having joined the Foundation more than a decade ago, he has played an important role in rebuilding the organisation, developing long-standing relationships and expanding its partnerships across the health and social development sectors.

One of THF's most significant contributions is its support of Save7, a national initiative addressing South Africa's critical shortage of organ donors. Through strategic guidance, governance oversight and relationship-building, THF has helped support the initiative's development and expansion. Save7's awareness campaigns have reached more than 10 million people, established six branches and mobilised over 400 volunteers. The initiative has also advanced innovative solutions, including the Kidney Exchange and Patient Support Architecture, aimed at improving support and outcomes for kidney patients.

Another important initiative is the Scaling Use of Data project, funded by the Gates Foundation. Working with provincial and City health departments in the Western Cape, the project promotes the use of routine healthcare data to improve tuberculosis (TB) and HIV outcomes. By helping healthcare managers and frontline teams identify service delivery gaps and make evidence-based decisions, the initiative strengthens healthcare management and supports improved patient care. THF acts as a funding conduit and programme partner, ensuring donor investment remains aligned with public-sector priorities and accountable management practices.

The Foundation has also extended its work into social development through its partnership with the Western Cape Department of Social Development to establish the Cape Care Fund. The initiative provides a transparent mechanism through which donors can support vetted organisations delivering essential community services. THF has contributed to the Fund's development by mobilising donor participation, strengthening partnerships and establishing responsible resource management processes.

Further demonstrating its commitment to evidence-informed development is the Cape Town Systemic Healthcare Action Research Project (C-SHARP), a node of the South African Population Research Infrastructure Network (SAPRIN). Since 2023, the project has assessed more than 30,000 households and registered over 100,000 individuals in Nomzamo and Bishop Lavis. This longitudinal data provides valuable insight into community health, social and economic conditions, supporting better-informed decisions, policy development and resource allocation.

The four initiatives show what that role looks like in practice. Save7 needs a network that can mobilise volunteers and increase public awareness. The Scaling Use of Data project helps health teams act on information they already collect. The Cape Care Fund creates a route for donors to reach vetted organisations, while C-SHARP builds evidence that can inform decisions over time. Across these projects, THF helps partners connect investment to specific public needs and establish who is responsible for delivery.

Best NGO · 2027 Finalist

The South African Red Cross
Society puts solar power to work
in humanitarian response

About this finalist

CategoryBest NGO
RepresentativeIreen Mutombwa
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

When basic services are disrupted, recovery can depend on practical questions such as where a family will get water and how it will keep essential activities going at home. The South African Red Cross Society (SARCS) is addressing those needs with solar-powered boreholes and household solar kits, linking emergency response with longer-term access to water and energy.

Guided by the Fundamental Principles of the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement, SARCS provides essential humanitarian services, including disaster preparedness and response, health and community care, first aid training and assistance to people affected by emergencies. Its work also incorporates Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS), recognising that recovery from hardship involves more than meeting physical needs. Supporting emotional wellbeing, restoring dignity and helping individuals cope with trauma are equally important in building healthy and resilient communities.

The Society describes this approach as a green humanitarian response. It reflects how unreliable infrastructure, limited household resources and climate-related disruptions can compound one another. The aim is to put solutions in place that communities can use in daily life and draw on when normal services are interrupted.

Through initiatives such as solar-powered boreholes and household solar kits, the organisation helps communities access safe water and reliable energy while reducing dependence on costly and unsustainable energy sources. These interventions are particularly valuable in areas where communities face disruptions to essential services, whether as a result of disasters, infrastructure challenges or economic hardship.

Solar-powered boreholes provide a more sustainable means of accessing water, supporting household needs and improving community wellbeing. Meanwhile, household solar kits offer families access to reliable energy, helping them maintain essential daily activities and reducing their vulnerability when conventional energy sources are unavailable. By combining these practical solutions with community engagement and inclusive approaches, SARCS is demonstrating how humanitarian assistance can contribute to longer-term development rather than simply responding to immediate crises.

Driving this work is Ireen Mutombwa, whose passion for humanitarian services is rooted in the opportunity to make a meaningful difference in the lives of people experiencing hardship and uncertainty. Through her work with SARCS, she has witnessed the importance of timely assistance, effective disaster response and psychosocial support in helping individuals and families recover, rebuild and regain hope.

For Mutombwa, some of the most meaningful achievements have been those that extend beyond immediate relief. Supporting disaster-affected families, contributing to MHPSS initiatives and helping implement sustainable interventions have demonstrated the importance of addressing the interconnected needs of vulnerable communities. Seeing people gain access to safe water, reliable energy and emotional support reinforces her belief that everyone deserves dignity, assistance and the opportunity to thrive, regardless of their circumstances.

The organisation's approach reflects a broader understanding of resilience: communities must not only be supported during emergencies but also equipped with the resources and capabilities to withstand future challenges. By integrating environmental sustainability, psychosocial wellbeing and community participation into its humanitarian work, SARCS is helping create stronger foundations for recovery and long-term stability.

The two interventions work at different scales. A borehole can serve a shared need for water, while a household kit addresses access to energy at home. That distinction matters when planning recovery. Restoring a community service is one part of the process. Families also need ways to resume routines and manage interruptions once immediate relief has ended.

Ultimately, the South African Red Cross Society's work demonstrates that humanitarian assistance is about more than responding when disaster strikes. It is about protecting dignity, strengthening resilience and ensuring communities have the support and resources they need to build a more secure future. Through its green humanitarian initiatives and continued commitment to vulnerable populations, SARCS is helping create lasting change across South Africa.

Best Rising NGO · 2027 Finalist

How YLED helps young people put
leadership into practice

About this finalist

CategoryBest Rising NGO
RepresentativeThando Zwane
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

A young person can recognise a problem in their community long before they have the confidence, contacts or practical skills to do something about it. Youth Leadership and Entrepreneurship Development (YLED) works in that gap. Its programmes give young people room to practise leading, make decisions and explore how an idea might become a business or a response to a local need.

For more than two decades, YLED has been working to bridge the gap between young people's potential and the opportunities available to them. Founded by Dr Steven Zwane, the organisation was inspired by his personal experiences and his recognition of the limited access to leadership development, mentorship and entrepreneurial opportunities faced by young people, particularly those from underserved communities. Having witnessed the barriers that prevent talented young people from realising their ambitions, Dr Zwane established YLED to help equip the next generation with the knowledge, confidence and practical tools needed to succeed.

At the heart of YLED's work is its commitment to developing well-rounded, confident and socially conscious young leaders. Through leadership development, entrepreneurship education, financial literacy and practical learning experiences, the organisation supports both in-school and out-of-school youth in preparing for opportunities in education, business and the workplace. Working alongside schools, communities and corporate partners, YLED creates inclusive environments where young people can identify their strengths, develop essential interpersonal skills and learn how to turn their ideas into meaningful opportunities.

Practical application sits alongside mentorship and personal development. Participants work on communicating ideas clearly, weighing financial decisions and taking responsibility for the choices they make. Entrepreneurial thinking gives these skills a purpose. A young person developing an idea has to explain who it serves, consider what it will take to carry out and listen to feedback. Those habits are valuable whether the participant goes on to start an enterprise, pursue further study or take a first job.

An important part of this journey is the leadership and dedication of the people driving the organisation forward. Among them is Thando Zwane, whose passion for youth development is rooted in a personal understanding of the challenges and opportunities that shape young people's lives. Growing up in a township, Thando witnessed first-hand the remarkable potential within communities, as well as the barriers that can prevent young people from accessing the support and exposure they need to succeed.

This experience continues to inform Thando's approach to leadership. Believing that talent and potential should never be determined by one's circumstances, Thando is driven by the opportunity to help young people recognise their strengths, build confidence and believe in what they can achieve. Beyond marketing and programme promotion, Thando has played an important role in strengthening YLED as an organisation, leading teams, improving operational systems, building partnerships and securing projects that support programme delivery.

Working with limited resources has required resilience, adaptability and a strong sense of purpose. However, the results make the journey worthwhile. In 2026, YLED celebrated 63 graduates, an achievement that reflects the collective commitment of its team, partners and the young people who have embraced the opportunities offered through its programmes.

The organisation's impact extends well beyond programme completion. Its ambition is to nurture a generation of young people who pursue further education, secure employment, establish businesses, become community leaders and contribute positively to society. By investing in young people today, YLED is helping to create opportunities that can have a lasting, generational impact.

The longer-term ambition is for participants to carry these skills into the places where they already have relationships and responsibilities. A participant who learns to make a case for an idea, organise others around it and follow through has a foundation for leading in a workplace, a business or a community project.

Best Rising NGO · 2027 Finalist

How Empowervate helps learners
tackle problems in their
communities

About this finalist

CategoryBest Rising NGO
RepresentativeAmanda Blankfield-Koseff
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

A group of Grade 10 learners in the Eastern Cape saw a problem in the river their community relied on. They turned it into a project that took them from a local clean-up to an international stage. Their story grew out of Empowervate’s Youth Citizens Action Programme, which gives learners a structured way to act on problems they encounter at school or at home.

Founded on the belief that young people are not simply the leaders of tomorrow but the change-makers of today, Empowervate challenges the traditional perception of youth as passive recipients of development. Instead, it places young people at the centre of finding solutions to the challenges affecting their communities. Through its programmes, the organisation demonstrates that when learners are given the right guidance, resources and opportunities, they can become powerful contributors to social change.

Empowervate has run the Youth Citizens Action Programme (YCAP) since 2009. Its ten-step process takes learners from identifying a concern to putting a response into practice and showing what it achieved. Each stage gives the team a decision to make. What is the problem? Who does it affect? What can the learners do with the resources and support available to them?

Serving learners from Grades 5 to 12, YCAP places particular emphasis on young people in rural, township and under-resourced communities. The programme develops essential life skills, including leadership, problem-solving, teamwork, critical thinking, communication, social responsibility and entrepreneurship. More importantly, it gives learners the opportunity to apply these skills in real-world situations, helping them understand that they have both the ability and responsibility to influence the environments in which they live.

One of the most inspiring examples of the programme's impact involved a team of Grade 10 learners from a rural community in the Eastern Cape. Identifying access to clean water as a significant challenge, the learners developed a river clean-up project that enabled them to purify contaminated river water, making it accessible to approximately 500 community members. Their remarkable achievement earned them the opportunity to represent South Africa at the G20 in Brazil, where their work received a standing ovation. The project illustrates the extraordinary potential of young people when they are encouraged to take initiative and given the tools to turn ideas into meaningful action.

Driving this vision is Amanda Blankfield-Koseff, whose passion for youth development is rooted in changing the narrative around young people and their role in society. For Amanda, one of the most rewarding aspects of the organisation's work is witnessing learners transform from being labelled as troublemakers into confident, responsible leaders who actively contribute to their communities. Many also discover talents and capabilities they did not previously recognise, carrying their newfound confidence and civic-mindedness into tertiary education and their future careers.

Empowervate's approach is particularly important in a country where inequality, unemployment and limited access to opportunities continue to affect young people's prospects. By helping learners recognise their agency and develop practical leadership skills, the organisation is investing in individuals who can influence not only their own futures but also those of the people around them.

Having to demonstrate what changed is an important part of the process. A team can use what it learns during delivery to judge whether its first idea fits the problem and where to adjust its plans. The experience helps learners practise the work of active citizenship, from agreeing on a priority to explaining the results to other people.

Empowervate aims to extend YCAP to more schools through partnerships that can support both participation and delivery. The programme starts with what learners know about their surroundings, so the same process can work across communities with very different concerns.

Through YCAP, the organisation is doing more than developing young leaders; it is demonstrating that young people already have the potential to change their communities. They simply need the opportunity, encouragement and practical support to make it happen.

Best Corporate CEO · 2027 Finalist

How Afrika Tikkun Bambanani
equips early learning centres

About this finalist

CategoryBest Corporate CEO
RepresentativeTheresa Michael
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

An early childhood development practitioner’s work is shaped by what is available in the room, what training they have received and what they can tell about each child’s progress. Afrika Tikkun Bambanani EdTech brings those pieces together through training, a structured curriculum, digital resources and developmental assessments for early learning centres in under-resourced communities.

Afrika Tikkun Bambanani is an innovative ECD organisation focused on reducing educational inequality by strengthening the quality of early learning in communities where resources and opportunities are often scarce. Its holistic approach supports children aged one to six through a combination of play-based learning, practitioner development, digital education, developmental assessments, leadership training and ongoing support for ECD centres.

Bambanani begins with the people and settings children encounter every day. The practitioner needs a way to plan activities, the centre needs resources to deliver them and both need a way to understand how a child is developing. Supporting those parts together gives educators a practical structure for improving what happens in the learning space.

In underserved communities, many ECD centres operate without structured curricula, adequate learning materials, technology or sufficiently trained practitioners. As a result, children may enter formal schooling without the foundational skills needed to participate confidently and succeed. Bambanani aims to address these challenges before learning gaps become more difficult to overcome.

Through its integrated model, practitioners receive training and ongoing guidance to strengthen their teaching practices, while digital learning tools help make quality educational content more accessible. Developmental assessments also provide insight into children's progress and help identify areas where additional support may be needed. By combining these interventions, the programme seeks to improve school readiness while creating stronger, more sustainable learning environments.

The scale of its work is already significant. Afrika Tikkun Bambanani has reached more than 825 ECD centres, supported 82,250 children and worked with 6,010 practitioners across South Africa. These numbers represent a growing network of educators and communities working together to give young children a better foundation for lifelong learning.

At the heart of this work is Theresa Michael, whose more than 25 years of experience in education have shaped her belief that every child deserves the opportunity to reach their full potential, regardless of their background. Michael is particularly passionate about empowering teachers and practitioners, recognising the important role they play in shaping young lives.

For her, some of the most rewarding moments are seeing an ECD centre transform from a struggling environment into a thriving place of learning, watching practitioners grow in confidence and witnessing children develop the skills they need for the next stage of their education. She views Bambanani as more than an educational programme; it is an opportunity to change lives, restore hope and create better prospects for future generations.

One of the organisation's proudest achievements has been its growth into a programme reaching thousands of educators and tens of thousands of children. Its impact has also attracted international recognition, including its inclusion in the HundrED Global Collection 2026 Top 100 education innovations.

That combination also helps explain why the technology belongs alongside training. A digital tool can make content available to an educator. Training helps the educator use it in ways that suit the children in the room, while assessments provide a way to observe progress and decide where more guidance may be needed.

By investing in the people, resources and environments that make early education possible, Afrika Tikkun Bambanani EdTech is helping ensure that a child's circumstances do not determine their potential. Its work demonstrates that stronger foundations in early childhood can create opportunities that last a lifetime.

Best Corporate CEO · 2027 Finalist

Digify Africa builds routes from
digital learning to paid work

About this finalist

CategoryBest Corporate CEO
RepresentativeQhakaza Mohare
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

Thabang Hlongwane joined Digify Africa without having completed matric. A placement at Mark1 Agency gave him a foothold in digital work. He later returned to Digify Africa as a service provider. That progression illustrates what the Black women-led non-profit social enterprise is trying to create through accessible training and links to employment.

Founded in 2012, Digify Africa was established around a fundamental belief: Africa does not lack talent; it lacks accessible routes to opportunity. Across South Africa, many young people face significant barriers to participating in the digital economy, including high data costs, limited connectivity, under-resourced schools and a lack of professional networks. Even for those who acquire qualifications, the transition from education to their first paid opportunity can remain challenging.

Digify Africa seeks to bridge these gaps by designing learning experiences around technology that people already use. Rather than relying on expensive platforms or high-speed internet connections, the organisation uses WhatsApp learning chatbots that operate on ordinary smartphones and require relatively little data. This approach enables it to reach young people, educators and learners who might otherwise be excluded from conventional digital education programmes.

Its work is delivered through three key programmes: Kitso, iLizwe Lam and Digify Pro. Together, these initiatives develop digital skills, digital citizenship and employability, helping participants build the capabilities needed to navigate an increasingly technology-driven world. By combining accessible learning with practical skills development, Digify Africa aims to create a pathway through which access becomes knowledge, knowledge becomes employment and graduates go on to create opportunities for others.

Many also needed academic guidance, emotional support, mentorship and access to networks that could help them navigate the journey beyond school. Siyandisa responded by broadening its approach, creating a more holistic support system designed to equip young people for long-term success.

Each year, the programme enrols 50 new learners, offering a range of interventions that include academic assistance, tutoring, psychosocial development, mentorship and expert-led webinars. These opportunities help learners strengthen their academic performance while developing the confidence, knowledge and practical skills needed to make informed decisions about their futures. Importantly, support does not end when learners leave school. Siyandisa continues walking alongside its Achievers through university and into their careers, helping them navigate the transitions that can often determine their future prospects.

The programme's impact is reflected in the 487 Achievers it has supported to date and its engagement with more than 200 schools across South Africa's nine provinces. These figures represent more than participation; they reflect young people being given the tools and encouragement to overcome barriers, pursue further education and build pathways towards meaningful employment and socio-economic mobility.

At the heart of this work is Cikizwa Bacela, whose commitment to creating opportunities for young people is rooted in a belief that circumstance should never determine a person's potential. Her motivation extends beyond academic achievement, focusing on the wider impact that education and access to opportunity can have on individuals, families and communities.

For Bacela, the most rewarding part of her work is witnessing the resilience and growth of the young people she has had the privilege of supporting. She describes her passion as being less about personal achievements and more about the lives she has been able to walk alongside. Seeing Achievers transform their futures, uplift their families and eventually contribute to the communities from which they came is what gives the programme its deeper purpose.

Her vision is also grounded in collaboration. By connecting talented learners with individuals and organisations that have the resources, expertise and networks to support them, Siyandisa helps bridge the divide between opportunity and those who have historically had limited access to it. Building on a decade of impact, the programme is now focused on expanding its reach, strengthening resource mobilisation and developing strategic partnerships to ensure that more deserving learners can benefit.

Taking in a new group each year gives Siyandisa a way to make that support recurrent. Tutoring helps with the demands learners face now, while mentorship and career exposure give them time to explore choices before they have to make them. For learners who have had little access to professional networks, those conversations can make unfamiliar routes easier to understand.

Ultimately, Siyandisa's greatest legacy may be found in the futures its Achievers go on to create. By investing in young people and supporting them well beyond the classroom, the programme is helping build a generation equipped not only to succeed individually, but also to strengthen the communities and country they call home.

How Vusa the Guardian makes
conservation part of children’s
reading

About this finalist

CategoryBest Social Enterprise
RepresentativeDesmond Langkilde
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

What does a child living near a protected area learn about the wildlife around them? The Southern African Conservation Trust (SACT) uses comics to make those animals and the issues around them easier to understand. Its Vusa the Guardian series brings conservation into a format that young readers can explore at school and share at home.

SACT's mission is to mitigate human-wildlife conflict by fostering harmonious, integrated ecosystems in which rural communities and wildlife can thrive together. The organisation recognises that conservation efforts cannot succeed in isolation from the people who share their environments with wild animals. Instead, lasting change requires local communities, particularly young people, to be actively involved in protecting the natural world.

To achieve this, SACT works across three key pillars: Education, Conflict Mitigation, and Research & Science. Together, these areas support a community-led approach to environmental change, addressing the causes of human-wildlife conflict while building greater awareness of the ecological importance of Southern Africa's wildlife.

A central part of the organisation's work is its educational comic initiative, developed in response to the need for accessible environmental learning in rural communities situated near wildlife areas. Traditional conservation messaging does not always reach these communities in ways that are engaging or relevant to younger audiences. SACT identified an opportunity to use storytelling and illustration to make important conservation messages easier for children to understand and remember.

The resulting comic series introduces young readers to the wildlife around them, explaining the importance of species such as lions, pangolins and vultures, while addressing issues including endangered species exploitation, responsible domestic animal care and the challenges of human-wildlife conflict. Distributed free of charge to rural schools, the comics encourage children to become active participants in conservation, rather than simply observers of it.

The initiative's flagship series, Vusa the Guardian, has already achieved a distribution reach of 134,500. Its revised Vusa the Dog Guardian comic is intended to extend this impact further, with an ambitious target of reaching more than one million children through schools and veterinary clinics. The wider educational collection now includes ten terrestrial titles and one marine title, demonstrating how the model can be adapted to different species, environments and conservation challenges.

The scale of this operation is significant. Between 2025 and 2026, SACT successfully coordinated the pre-press production, printing and regional distribution of more than 300,000 educational comics to primary schools and partner non-profit organisations. This logistics model demonstrates the initiative's potential to be replicated and expanded cost-effectively, bringing conservation education to communities across the region.

Behind this work is Desmond Langkilde, whose commitment to conservation is closely connected to his passion for storytelling, illustration and accessible education. Langkilde believes that empowering rural communities is essential to achieving meaningful, long-term environmental protection. His focus is on transforming young people living alongside wildlife areas into informed custodians of their natural heritage, helping replace conflict with understanding and sustainable coexistence.

His contribution extends beyond the comic series. Langkilde has also directed the Shepherd's Shield initiative, which provides insurance and equipment to frontline Animal Protection Unit rangers, and supported the expansion of Wild at HeART workshops to empower young people across borders.

The settings for distribution are part of the thinking behind the project. Schools provide a way to reach readers as they are building their understanding of wildlife. Veterinary clinics offer a setting relevant to the dog-focused edition. Both give the comics a life beyond printing, putting the material where readers and adults can discuss it together.

The Trust’s wider work connects what children learn on the page to the practical decisions people face when they live near wildlife. Education can help families understand why an animal matters, while conflict mitigation helps address the risks that come with sharing space.

Best Green Impact Initiative · 2027 Finalist

Urban Surfer builds recycling
infrastructure around waste
reclaimers

About this finalist

CategoryBest Green Impact Initiative
RepresentativeSifiso Gumbi
Edition2027 CSI Legacy Awards

Informal waste reclaimers keep glass and other recyclable materials out of landfills, often while working without a safe place to sort what they collect. Urban Surfer’s first formalised reclaimer hub, in Parktown, tackles that gap. Its equipment and organised workspace are designed around the people who recover the materials the recycling sector depends on.

Founded by Sifiso Gumbi, a former waste picker from Soweto, Urban Surfer was established to address the challenges faced by informal waste reclaimers while creating more effective ways for businesses and municipalities to recover recyclable materials. The organisation works across the recycling value chain, establishing formalised recycling hubs, providing reclaimers with equipment and training, and helping businesses separate waste at source and monitor their recycling activities.

At the heart of its approach is the belief that environmental progress and social inclusion should go hand in hand. Diverting waste from landfill is important, but the people responsible for collecting and sorting those materials must also benefit from the value they create. Urban Surfer aims to improve working conditions, provide more reliable income opportunities and ensure reclaimers have a voice in decisions that affect recycling in their communities.

The initiative is deeply personal for Gumbi, whose journey into entrepreneurship began while living at Mzimhlophe Hostel in Soweto. Collecting recyclables provided him with an income, but it also exposed him to the difficulties experienced by people working informally in the sector. Reclaimers often spent long days collecting materials without knowing what they would earn, while working without adequate equipment or secure facilities in which to sort and store their collections.

Gumbi also recognised the significant environmental contribution reclaimers were making, often without acknowledgement or fair compensation. Rather than viewing waste collection simply as an informal survival activity, he saw the potential to create a structured system that connected reclaimers with businesses and municipalities, giving them better access to markets and opportunities to grow their own enterprises.

One of Urban Surfer's most significant milestones has been opening its first formalised reclaimer hub in Parktown. The facility provides reclaimers with a safer, more organised working environment, supported by equipment including a solar-powered baler and a weighbridge. For Gumbi, the hub represents the kind of progress he once hoped to see when he was collecting recyclables himself. He still keeps his overalls in his car, enjoying opportunities to return to the sites and work alongside reclaimers. These experiences keep him connected to the realities of the sector and the practical changes that can make a difference to people's livelihoods.

Urban Surfer's environmental impact is also demonstrated through its glass incentive programme, which has helped reclaimers collect more than three million kilograms of glass. By creating incentives and strengthening access to recycling markets, the programme encourages greater recovery of recyclable materials while supporting the people who collect them.

Gumbi's contribution to environmental innovation has received wider recognition. He has been named SAYA Young Environmentalist of the Year, won the Eco-Logic Award for Ecological Innovation two years running, and received PETCO's Entrepreneur of the Year award in 2025. He has also shared his journey and vision at TEDxJohannesburg Countdown.

A weighbridge helps make the weight of collected material visible, and a baler allows material to be compacted for handling. These are practical improvements to the steps between collecting recyclables and getting them into the recycling market. The Parktown hub puts that infrastructure together in one place, while the glass incentive programme addresses what people earn for the material they recover.

Urban Surfer demonstrates that environmental sustainability is strongest when the people closest to the challenge are part of the solution. By combining practical recycling infrastructure with economic opportunity and greater recognition for informal reclaimers, the enterprise is helping create a future in which both communities and the environment can benefit.
